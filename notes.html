Periodic Trends — Notes

Year 10 Chemistry — covers what the interactive app shows you directly. Use it alongside the app, not instead of it.

1Subatomic particles

Every atom is built from three particles:

  • Proton — positively charged, found in the nucleus. The number of protons is the atomic number, and it's what makes an element that element.
  • Neutron — no charge, also in the nucleus, adds mass. Atoms of the same element can have different neutron counts — these are called isotopes.
  • Electron — negatively charged, found in shells around the nucleus. The outermost ("valence") electrons are what drive an atom's chemistry.

An atom is neutral overall, so the number of electrons always equals the number of protons — unless it's become an ion (more on that elsewhere).

2How the periodic table is organised

Elements are arranged so that similar elements line up in columns. Metals sit on the left and middle of the table, nonmetals sit on the right, and metalloids form a staircase boundary between them.

Metal properties: shiny, malleable (bendable), good conductors of heat/electricity, tend to lose electrons.
Nonmetal properties: dull, brittle if solid, poor conductors, tend to gain electrons.
Metalloid properties: in between — often semiconductors (e.g. silicon).

3Periods and groups

A period is a horizontal row. A group is a vertical column. Moving across a period keeps the same number of shells; moving down a group adds a shell.

Key groups to know

  • Group 1 — Alkali metals (Li, Na, K, Rb, Cs, Fr): 1 valence electron, extremely reactive, reactivity increases down the group.
  • Group 2 — Alkaline-earth metals (Be, Mg, Ca, Sr, Ba, Ra): 2 valence electrons, reactive but less so than Group 1.
  • Group 17 — Halogens (F, Cl, Br, I, At): 7 valence electrons, very reactive nonmetals, reactivity decreases down the group.
  • Group 18 — Noble gases (He, Ne, Ar, Kr, Xe, Rn): full outer shell, essentially unreactive.

4Why elements in a group behave alike

Elements in the same group have the same number of valence electrons, and chemical behaviour is driven almost entirely by valence electrons. That's why all the halogens react in similar ways, all the noble gases are unreactive, and so on — it's not a coincidence, it's the shared outer-shell count.

5Atomic radius trends

Down a group: atomic radius increases. Each row down adds a whole new shell, and each shell sits further from the nucleus than the last — so the atom gets bigger, even though the nuclear charge is also increasing.

Across a period: atomic radius decreases. The number of shells stays the same, but protons are being added to the nucleus every step, so the nuclear charge pulls all the electrons — including the outer ones — in more tightly.

In the app: watch the atom itself visibly grow or shrink as you move. That resize isn't just decorative — it's modelling this exact trend.

6Reading shells and valence electrons from the table

For main-group elements, the period number tells you how many shells the atom has, and the group number tells you the valence electron count (for groups 1–2, it's the group number; for groups 13–18, it's the group number minus 10).

Chlorine is period 3, group 17 → 3 shells, 7 valence electrons → shell configuration 2, 8, 7.

7Stability and the octet rule

An atom is considered stable when its outer (valence) shell is full — 8 electrons for most atoms (2 for a first-shell-only atom like helium). Full outer shell means very low reactivity, which is exactly why noble gases barely react with anything.

Atoms with an outer shell that's nearly full or nearly empty are the most reactive, because they only need to lose or gain a small number of electrons to reach that stable full-shell state.

8Drawing a Bohr model

A Bohr diagram shows the nucleus (protons + neutrons) at the centre, surrounded by concentric shells, each holding electrons up to its capacity (2 in the first shell, 8 in the next few). To draw one:

  • Find the atomic number → that's your proton count and electron count.
  • Find the mass number − atomic number → that's your neutron count.
  • Fill shells from the inside out, 2 then 8 then 8 (for the elements you'll typically be asked to draw).

9Reactivity and the bottom-left of the table

The most reactive metals are found in the bottom-left of the periodic table (think francium, caesium). Two trends stack on top of each other here: being further down a group means the valence electron is further from the nucleus (weaker attraction, easier to remove), and being in Group 1 means there's only one valence electron to begin with. Combine those and you get the most reactive metals on the whole table.
